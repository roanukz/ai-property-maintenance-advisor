Three minutes

Summary

  • The problem. An owner who lives far from a rental property hears about a broken hot tub or air conditioner as one sentence from a guest, and the technician's first visit often goes to finding out which part is needed (Part 1). The first version of this tool (v1) turned a photo of the equipment's data plate, the label with its make, model and serial number, into a brief cited to the maker's documents, and refused when the documents did not support an answer. But v1 remembered nothing: every brief paid for 7 to 8 fresh web searches, even for a unit it had researched before, and it knew nothing about the property (Part 3, Part 4).
  • The thesis. A tool that remembers what it has already verified, and knows each property's records, can answer a repeat question with fewer searches and for less money without giving up any of v1's guarantees, as long as those guarantees stay in code rather than in the instructions given to the model. That needed a program that can branch, loop, pause for a person and save its progress, which is what LangGraph (a library that runs an AI workflow as a graph of steps) provides; LangChain (a library of building blocks for AI apps) runs the research step inside it (Part 5). It also needed each step to go to the cheapest tool that can do it: Anthropic's Claude for reading the plate, choosing what to search and writing the brief, and Tavily, a web search service built for AI tools, on its free tier for search (Part 6).
  • The method. The bars were written into the product requirements on 17 September 2026, before any test ran: a repeat question about the same unit uses 2 searches or fewer, compared with the same unit's first v2 lookup; an invented model is refused 3 times out of 3 on the cheaper model; no cheap mode brief spends more than $0.15 and the whole build no more than $5; speed is reported, with no target. Replay (running the whole pipeline on recorded model replies and recorded search results, at no cost) tests the rules, the routing and the spending caps. Live batches on Claude Haiku 5.5 and Tavily, run on 13 and 14 October 2026, test how the model behaves; before ten new first lookups ran, a further bar was set: at least 9 of 10 must answer. A differential test sends 180 test briefs through both v1's validator (the code that checks each brief against the rules) and the rewrite's, and compares the verdicts (Part 8). Safety step flagging was tested on 29 and 30 September against 345 steps labeled by three model readers before any scoring, with the rule for choosing what ships written before any result. On 15 October 2026 I labeled the 175 held out steps myself, blind, with my answers sealed before any score, and Claude Haiku 5.5 was asked the same question as Jev, a judgment model from TypeSafe that flags safety steps, in a comparison whose settings were fixed before it ran (Part 9).
  • The results. On Claude Haiku 5.5 (build d24ed7c475dcd813), a repeat of the hot tub's code question was answered from memory with 0 searches, against 2 on its first lookup. The other two repeats ended with no reliable answer: a vague hot tub question found the stored code but no documented causes, so it ran a short top up (a capped extra search pass when memory holds something but not enough) of 2 searches whose limit blocked the owner's manual it needed, and a new air conditioner question ran 4 searches because that unit's first lookup had also ended with no reliable answer and saved nothing: a miss on the bar of 2 or fewer. v1 ran 7 to 8 on every brief. The invented model was refused 3 of 3 times, each refusal made by the model. Of the ten new first lookups, 7 answered, against the bar of 9; Haiku 4.5 had answered all 10 (Part 8). A first lookup cost $0.0075 and answers from memory $0.0015 and $0.0013, against the $0.15 cap and an estimated $0.35 to $0.71 for a v1 brief; they took 40.1, 9.8 and 7.7 seconds, against 57 to 117 seconds for v1. A live run with synthetic property records cited the unit's earlier service record and its age, and a line written by code, not the model, said none of its warranty coverage was active. A replacement sweep, which checks each unit for a newer model its maker names, verified no successor for any of the three real units, and the product recall check found no product recall naming any of their exact models (Part 7). Offline, v1's and v2's validators agreed on all 180 test briefs. Jev flagged 30 of the 31 safety steps the model readers marked in the half of the labeled set kept aside for the final score, against 20 for a rule matching the words breaker, power and heat. By my own blind labels it flagged 22 of 26, against 18 for either word rule and 19 for Claude Haiku 5.5 asked the same question, and about half of its 41 flags were steps I would not call safety steps. On the ten new lookups every safety step in a brief was flagged, 11 of 11 (Part 9).
  • What it means. Remembering pays where it was designed to: an answer from memory cost about a fifth of a first lookup and took about a quarter of the time. But memory holds only what a first lookup verified: it keeps a code's meaning, not the steps, so both answers from memory listed no steps to try first where the first brief listed 4, and a first lookup that ends with no reliable answer saves nothing for the next question. The move to Claude Haiku 5.5 cut the cost per run to about a sixth of Haiku 4.5's, made runs slower and answered fewer first lookups. Haiku 4.5's 10 of 10 included answers built on the maker's general troubleshooting pages, which the tool's own rule does not allow; Haiku 5.5 more often declined on such pages, though not always, and no code enforces that rule. None of these changes loosens a rule that runs in code, and the tests cannot show that a cheaper brief is as good. On the ten new lookups the writing model flagged 10 of the 11 safety steps by itself, and the code check by Jev added the eleventh. Jev still catches the most safety steps by a person's reading, but with 26 safety steps its lead over the word lists and over Claude sits inside overlapping 95% intervals (the range each true rate probably falls in), so whether it beats a word list is not settled (Part 9).
  • Cautions, and how to check. Replay proves the rules, the routing, the spending caps and the page layout; it cannot prove how the model behaves. The live samples are small: three runs for the refusal test, three repeat questions and ten first lookups, and three refusals out of three is consistent with a true refusal rate as low as about 37%. The property records and warranty rows are synthetic, and no technician has used a brief yet. Every live v2 number here comes from a named run on build d24ed7c475dcd813 (a build is a single frozen version of the code, identified by a fingerprint stored in every run record), run on 13 and 14 October 2026, US Eastern time (all on 14 October in UTC, the clock the run records use), with these exceptions: the product recall check ran on the evening of 13 October, before the ten first lookups, on build ad11e6e15a55d30a, which differs only in a research setting the check never reads; Haiku 4.5's figures, given for comparison, come from builds da738a1559ced359 and 9a64835e86a5a6c8; the labeled set scores trace to a locked settings file and the Jev model version (jev-1.13.0), not to a build; the Claude comparison ran on 15 October 2026 on build 179b4295c98a3143, which differs from d24ed7c475dcd813 only in a command that prints memory statistics; v1's come from its own live run of 18 August 2026; and the whole build spend also counts runs on earlier builds. The safety step labels came first from three model readers, which were Claude models and may favor Claude in the comparison. The check against a person's labels has now been run, but the person is me, one labeler and not a technician, and 26 safety steps is a small sample. On 15 October 2026, all 3,542 offline tests passed on the code of build 179b4295c98a3143, with the network blocked, no key and no cost (Appendix A).

Part 1

The problem

The property is an eight bedroom lake rental with a hot tub, two heating and air conditioning (HVAC) systems, a septic system, and a dock. The owner lives ninety miles away. When something breaks, the report arrives as a sentence from a guest or a cleaner: the hot tub is not heating, the upstairs is warm. That sentence is everything the technician gets. It does not carry the model, the age of the unit, the code on the panel, or what was tried the last time this happened. So the first visit is often diagnostic rather than corrective, and the part comes on the second one.

The job: when equipment fails at a property I am not at, help me tell whoever is going to fix it what this thing is and what the documentation says about this symptom, so they arrive with the right part instead of finding out what they need.

Note the shape of that job. It is not "diagnose the fault." Nobody standing ninety miles away, and no tool, should be doing that. It is "carry the documentation to the person who can."

Part 2

What I actually know

Verified.

  • The failure this addresses is an information failure. Aberdeen Group research, cited widely across field service writing, puts about 51 percent of repeat visits down to a missing or wrong part, against 25 percent for technician experience and 13 percent for a job scheduled too short. I am citing secondary sources quoting Aberdeen rather than the original report, which I could not reach. Source, second source.
  • The operations tools in this category track the schedule, not the house. One of the leading products stores make, model, and serial as fields and does nothing further with them.
  • Manufacturer error code tables for this equipment exist publicly and are findable. The Sundance 880 series documentation the tool retrieves is real.

Inferred, and worth saying so.

  • That a cited brief reduces second visits. This is the thesis, and it is not measured.
  • That owners will photograph a data plate. It takes ten seconds, but nobody has been asked to do it yet.

Not known.

  • Whether a technician changes what they load in the van because a brief told them to. One tradesperson answering that converts this from a build into a finding. Version two does not answer it either.

The demand signal I found pointed somewhere slightly different: local forums are full of owners asking who to call, not what is wrong. I noted it, kept building the narrower thing, and left the larger question open rather than quietly redefining the product to match the evidence I happened to find.

Part 3

What v1 proved

Every decision in both versions follows from one: the tool is allowed to say it does not know, and that is a success state, not an error. A maintenance tool that returns a confident, wrong, plausible looking repair instruction sends someone to open an equipment bay on live electrical, or to order a part that was never the problem. So the product has a NO RELIABLE ANSWER state, with its own layout showing what was searched, what was found, and why that was not enough.

A tool that is right most of the time and silent about which times is not a tool a technician can use.

v1 worked like this: photograph the plate, confirm what was read, get a brief. It made two calls to Claude Opus 5, the highest priced of the three Claude models priced in the build, using Anthropic's own built in web search capped at 8 searches, and kept no database. Five decisions shaped it, and all carry into v2.

Decision 1

The refusal lives in code, not in the prompt

Risk addressed: trust

Instead of writing "refuse when sources are insufficient" into the instructions and trusting it, validation code clears any candidates and suggested steps when the state is NO RELIABLE ANSWER, whatever the model returned.

  • CostMore code, and a schema (the exact shape the answer must have) that the model must satisfy exactly.
  • BoughtThe promise holds on a bad day, when the model is having one.
Decision 2

Label every source honestly, including when the honest label is weak

Risk addressed: trust, and usefulness

Sources are tiered manufacturer, dealer, or forum. A dealer page stays labeled dealer even when it is the best source found, and a brief built only on forum content says so at the top.

  • CostSome briefs look thinner than a tool that quietly promoted its sources.
  • BoughtThe label means something, so a technician can weigh it.
Decision 3

An observed code stops the search from widening

Risk addressed: usefulness

If the symptom includes a code visible on the panel, the code overrides symptom matching. On the same hot tub, the vague symptom returned six documented possibilities and the observed code returned one. A list of six is research. One is a part number.

  • CostA misread code produces a confident single answer, so the brief tells the reader to confirm it on the unit.
  • BoughtThe difference between a technician researching and a technician packing.
Decision 4

Search at the moment of the question, instead of building a corpus first

Risk addressed: feasibility

An earlier design parsed manuals into a knowledge pack in advance, and acquiring primary sources at scale was its open problem. Searching live at request time inverted that: the corpus would become a byproduct of questions people actually asked.

  • CostEach brief costs money and time, and quality depends on what is public.
  • BoughtA working, testable product instead of a data acquisition project with no end date.
Decision 5

Never diagnose. Carry documentation instead.

Risk addressed: safety, and liability

The brief never states a cause. It lists documented candidates, each with the documented meaning, the documented action, who should do it, and why it surfaced. First steps must be documented, safe for an untrained person, and actionable. Anything electrical or heat related is flagged and sorted to the top.

  • CostReads as less impressive than a tool that names the fault.
  • BoughtIt stays inside what the sources support, which is the only place it has any authority.

Part 4

Where v1 stopped

Running v1 showed three limits that no prompt could fix, because each is about what the tool remembers and knows, not about how well the model writes.

  • It forgot everything. Every brief ran 7 to 8 live searches and read 42,000 to 91,000 tokens of input (a token is a word fragment, the unit models are billed in). A second question about the same hot tub paid the full price again. v1 logged every lookup but never stored what it verified.
  • It knew nothing about the property. The brief has a "this has happened before" field. It was empty in all 4 recorded v1 briefs, because v1 had no service history to fill it from.
  • It had no path for aging equipment. When a part is discontinued, the useful answer is a documented successor model, and v1 had nowhere to put one.

Cost made the first limit sharper. v1 never computed its own cost; its logged usage, priced at current list prices, puts a v1 brief at an estimated $0.35 to $0.71 (Part 6). Version two sets out to remember what it has verified, to know each property, and to spend less per brief, without moving any of the five decisions above out of code. Two items from v1's cut list come back for that reason: several properties, and warranty tracking.

Part 5

Why LangGraph and LangChain

LangGraph is a library that runs an AI workflow as a graph of steps. Each step reads and writes a shared record of the run, and arrows between the steps, called edges, decide what runs next. LangChain is a library of building blocks for AI apps: connectors to model providers, tools, and a ready made agent loop in which the model calls a tool, reads the result, and decides what to do next.

v1 was a fixed sequence of steps, and plain code was the right tool for it. v2 adds four things a fixed sequence cannot express cleanly: routing (answer from memory, top up with a few searches, or research in full), a bounded retry loop, a pause for a person to confirm the unit, and saved state for each property. Those four are a real reason to use a graph runtime, not a way to show off a framework.

The split: LangGraph runs the flow, LangChain runs one step

LangChain's ready made agent, create_agent, was considered for the whole flow and rejected: it cannot hold a confirmation pause that survives the program being closed and started again, the validation retry loop, or state kept per property. So LangGraph runs the whole flow, and create_agent runs only the research step inside it, where a model and tool loop is exactly what is needed.

What each capability is, and what the owner or technician gets from it
CapabilityWhat it meansWhat the user gets
Interrupt and checkpoint (LangGraph) An interrupt is a deliberate pause that waits for a person's answer. A checkpoint is a saved snapshot of the run, written after each step to a small database file. The owner checks the unit and the code before anything is spent on research, and can finish later, even after a restart, without paying for the plate reading twice.
Conditional edges (LangGraph) Arrows whose destination is chosen by a small function. The tool answers what it already knows and searches only when it has to. A failed check gets one smaller second pass, only if the budget allows.
Parallel branches and typed state (LangGraph) Several steps run at once, and the run's record has a fixed shape with a merge rule for each value they write. Service history, stored facts and live research are gathered together, and the cost and the "what was searched" list on each brief include every call.
create_agent and middleware (LangChain) The ready made loop of model and tools, and code that wraps every call in it and can stop a call before it happens. A known worst case for each lookup: at most 5 searches and 3 page fetches on the first pass, and 2 more searches on the one smaller retry (v1 allowed 8 searches), and no model call once the money is gone.
Structured output (LangChain) The model answers in a fixed, typed form, like filling in a form, not in free text. Every citation in a brief points to a page the tool actually retrieved, because the model can refer to a source only by its number and code builds the list.
Provider connectors (LangChain) Ready made connections to Claude and to Tavily's search and fetch. Search runs on a free tier while Claude does only the judgment, so the owner pays for one model at its cheapest adequate setting, and the recorded demo shows exactly the calls that were made live.

The tradeoffs actually paid

  • The names move fast. Many functions were renamed or moved after the 1.0 releases, and the documentation and the source code disagree in three places. Every name was checked against the current release, the source was taken as correct, and v2 relies on none of the three disputed behaviors.
  • No stock fake model could drive the agent. A fake model returns scripted replies so tests cost nothing. None of LangChain's supports tool calling (the model asking the code to run a search or fetch), and one silently loops back to the start of its script. v2 wrote its own, which fails loudly. Because it never checks the form the way the real API does, one paid check covered that gap.
  • The structured output ceiling. Anthropic limits a form to 24 optional and 16 either or fields, and LangChain passes the form through unchanged. A draft modeled on v1's lenient form would have failed on the first paid call, so the form is now flat, with every field required.
  • Defaults that had to be overridden. The agent's loop limit defaults to 9,999 steps, so the middleware caps are the only real guard; LangChain's own search limit leaves no reason in the run's record, so v2 wrote two small middleware pieces of its own. The Claude connector defaults to the model's maximum output, which on Haiku 4.5 would have reserved $0.32 against a $0.15 cap, so every call sets its own output limit, and neither connector has a timeout until one is set.
  • A rewrite in a second language. v1 is TypeScript and v2 is Python, because Python has the more complete LangGraph ecosystem (an inference, not a measurement). The price was porting v1's validator; the differential test pays it back (Part 8).
  • Model lifetime. Anthropic listed Haiku 4.5 as retiring no sooner than 15 October 2026, and the live command refuses a model's runs from that date. So cheap mode moved to Claude Haiku 5.5, listed as retiring no sooner than 7 October 2027, and every live Claude result the page reports as current was measured again on it, with Haiku 4.5's figures kept only for comparison (Part 8).

Alternatives rejected

  • A vector store beside the graph. It finds text by similarity of meaning. In my earlier project on documentation gaps, vector search scored 24 correct against 23 for graph retrieval, a gap too small to justify a third way to look things up.
  • A hosted graph database. At the size this build was planned for (about 50 nodes, an estimate), a Python library that holds the graph in memory and saves it as a plain file does the same job, with no account and nothing to pay.
  • Building the corpus up front. v1 already rejected it. v2 grows memory only from lookups that really happened, so early questions pay for full research.

Part 6

Two APIs, each for its job

An API is a service the code calls over the internet and pays for by use. v1 used one API for everything: Claude Opus 5 read the plate, ran Anthropic's own web search, and wrote the brief. v2 splits the work by what each step actually needs. Since 30 September a third service, TypeSafe's Jev, also checks each step to try first in a brief for safety (Part 9), and the product recall check asks a free government service, with no key and no cost (Part 7).

Claude, for judgment

  • Reads the plate photo.
  • Decides what to search and which pages to fetch.
  • Classifies a vague symptom, only when the routing rules cannot decide.
  • Writes the brief.
  • Cheap mode, the one run live, uses Claude Haiku 5.5, the lowest priced of the three models priced in the build, for every step, with research effort (a setting for how much the model thinks and how long it keeps researching) set to high. Full mode hands only the writing to Sonnet 5; it is tested in replay only.

Tavily, for finding pages

  • Runs the web searches and fetches page text.
  • Free tier: 1,000 credits a month. A basic search costs 1 credit; the 2 credit advanced search is switched off.
  • A page fetch costs 1 credit per 5 successful pages; failed fetches are free.
  • Paid overage is switched off, so a search costs $0.

Why the split is cheaper

Per million tokens, Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 in and $0.50 out on prompts up to 100,000 tokens ($0.50 and $2.50 above that), Sonnet 5 costs $2 and $10, and Opus 5 costs $5 and $25 (Anthropic's pricing page, checked 13 October 2026); Anthropic's server search adds $0.01 per search. Haiku 4.5, which the earlier live runs used, cost $1 and $5. So on shorter prompts the model price alone is a fiftieth of Opus 5's, and the search line, an estimated $0.07 to $0.08 per v1 brief (7 to 8 searches at $0.01), falls to $0 on Tavily's free tier. The $0 holds only on the free tier: at Tavily's pay as you go rate of $0.008 a credit, a worst case first pass of 8 credits would cost about $0.064 (arithmetic, not measured). So at volume most of the saving comes from the model and from memory skipping search.

The second payoff: the rules stay in code

In v1, Anthropic's built in search returned its citations in a way that did not work together with structured output, so v1 could not have code build the source list; it relied on an instruction to the model not to pad the bibliography. In v2, the search and fetch wrappers hand code the raw results. Code builds the source list only from searches and fetches that actually succeeded, the model can point to a source only by its number, and because code holds the raw page text it can check that every quote appears word for word on its page and that a cited error code appears there as a whole word. The instruction became a rule enforced in code.

How LangChain and LangGraph carry the split

  • LangChain puts both providers in one loop. The agent runs Claude's tool calling over Tavily's search and fetch, and one configuration file names the model for each step.
  • Middleware counts both bills in one place. It counts Tavily searches and fetches against their limits and reserves each Claude call in a spending ledger before the call is made.
  • LangGraph decides whether either API is called at all. Routing skips search when memory holds enough verified facts and sends a top up of 2 searches when it holds some. The confirmation pause sits before any research spend, so the only paid call before the owner confirms is the plate reading, which cost $0.0005 and $0.0006 live on build d24ed7c475dcd813.

The tradeoffs. Citation mapping, the page evidence checks and the credit count all became v2's own code, which is more to maintain than one provider, and it is the part that keeps the source list honest. The second tradeoff is quality: Haiku was picked because it is cheapest, and a cheaper model can write a less complete brief while every rule passes. So "cheaper" and "as good" are separate claims, and this page supports only the first (Part 9).

Part 7

What v2 lets owners and technicians do

Each feature is described by what the person gets and why it matters on the day something breaks. Where a live run shows it working, the demo case that replays it is named. The recorded v2 demo replays eight live runs of 14 October 2026 on build d24ed7c475dcd813. Four of the eight (cases 3, 4, 5 and 6) end with no reliable answer, and the demo shows them as refusals, the success state when the documents do not support an answer, though for the three real unit cases (3, 4 and 5) each also counts as a miss in Part 8. It shows warranty status on case 8, but not the replacement sweep or the product recall check.

Feature 1

Remember equipment it has already researched

For: the owner paying for the lookup, and the technician waiting on it

v2 keeps a knowledge graph: facts stored as things and the links between them, such as "this model shows this code", "this model has this documented cause", "this model replaced that one". Every fact carries its page address, the date it was retrieved, and the exact sentence from the page that supports it, and is stored only from a validated brief on a live run.

Live on build d24ed7c475dcd813, a repeat of the hot tub's FLO code question (FLO is the panel's flow switch code) was answered from memory with no search, for $0.0015 in 9.8 seconds, against $0.0075 and 40.1 seconds for its first lookup (demo cases 1 and 2). The repeat brief was thinner, though: memory keeps a code's documented meaning, not the steps around it, so the brief gave the code and its meaning but none of the 4 steps to try first that the first brief listed.

The air conditioner shows the other side. Its first lookup found Trane's general troubleshooting page and installer guides for other model numbers, but nothing documenting this model or its family, so the model declined and nothing was saved. A new question about the unit, a fan that would not spin, found nothing in memory, ran full research with 4 searches, and also ended with no reliable answer: $0.0089 in 43.5 seconds, against $0.0086 and 37.6 seconds for the first lookup (demo cases 4 and 5). On Haiku 4.5 (build da738a1559ced359) the same first lookup had answered, partly from Trane's general pages, and stored 6 documented causes.

  • GetsA repeat question about a unit the tool has already answered skips or shortens search, so it is faster and cheaper, though an answer from memory alone can be thinner.
  • WhyRental properties see the same equipment fail again, and v1 paid full price every time.
Feature 2

Know the property: history, age and warranty

For: the technician, before the visit

A matching earlier service record fills "this has happened before", cited to that record; a record for a different appliance is thrown out. Code computes the unit's age from the install date in the records, and the model is told not to. Warranty coverage is stored as one row per component, with its term and whether it starts at install or purchase, and code works out each end date and whether it is active on the run's date. The model never writes a warranty date, and the validator rejects one if it tries. The brief lists active coverage and tells the owner to check with the maker before paying for a repair; it never says which coverage applies, and with no rows it says "No warranty on file," never "out of warranty."

Shown live, on synthetic records. A live run on the hot tub with seeded synthetic property records attached (the model and the documentation were real; the records were made up for testing and are labeled so) cited the earlier synthetic service record, stated the unit was installed on 10 June 2021 and about 5 years old, and printed a coverage line written by code from three synthetic rows, all ended: "No warranty coverage on file is active as of 2026-10-14 (UTC)." It used 0 searches and cost $0.0013 in 7.7 seconds (run t-044cd20f9678498e on build d24ed7c475dcd813, demo case 8; its brief is briefs/v2/t-044cd20f9678498e.html). Like the repeat, it listed no steps to try first. Because every seeded term has ended, one synthetic water softener with active coverage was added; it shows on the offline property page, not in a live brief.

  • GetsWhat was tried last time, how old the unit is, and whether it is under warranty, on one page.
  • WhyThat is the context the one sentence report from a guest never carries.
Feature 3

A confirmation pause before any money is spent

For: the owner

After the plate is read, the run pauses. The owner confirms or corrects the model and the code. If the model number is unreadable, the run stays paused until someone types it, so a guess is never treated as confirmed. Live on build d24ed7c475dcd813, the clear plate was read exactly, every field at high confidence, and the blurry plate came back with its model, serial and date marked unreadable rather than guessed, and only the maker read, at low confidence (demo case 7).

  • GetsA check on what was read, before the lookup is paid for.
  • WhyA wrong model or a misread code wastes the whole lookup and can point to the wrong part.
Feature 4

Spending capped in code, and reported honestly

For: the owner paying the bill

Every paid call is reserved in a ledger (a running record of spend) before it is made, so a cheap mode brief stops at its $0.15 cap, give or take one call's estimate error. The ledger is never deleted, so a reset cannot grant a fresh budget. Running out of money is its own "budget stopped" status and never counts as NO RELIABLE ANSWER, because an empty wallet is not a finding about the documentation.

  • GetsA known worst case per brief.
  • WhyA tool that can run up an open bill is not one an owner leaves running.
Feature 5

The refusal and the honest labels, kept

For: the technician deciding what to trust

NO RELIABLE ANSWER is still enforced in validation code, and code can lower a source's tier label but never raise one. The badge now names the website that hosted the document beside it, so a maker's manual found on a third party archive is not a surprise. Live on build d24ed7c475dcd813, the invented model was refused on all 3 runs, each time by the model itself after it had reached the brand's real documentation (demo case 6).

  • GetsThe same guarantees as v1, plus the host behind every badge.
  • WhyCheaper is only useful if the brief is still safe to act on.
Feature 6

Upgrade options, replacement planning and maintenance, cited or absent

For: the owner deciding whether to repair, and planning prevention

A successor model appears only when a fetched page names it as a whole word ("Optima 880" is not taken from "Optima 880X"), and a price only when the cited page states that exact amount. A maintenance interval is kept only from the manufacturer, and code computes the due date from the property's maintenance log. Upgrade options and maintenance intervals are tested in replay only: in the re-recorded live runs, no brief needed an upgrade option or quoted a maintenance interval.

A replacement sweep now plans ahead of a failure. For each appliance at a property it shows the unit's age from its install date and any successor model a verified quote names; with nothing verified it says "No discontinuation documented in what this tool has verified," never "not discontinued." It reads stored facts at no cost, and an optional lookup asks only whether a model is discontinued and what replaced it, under one $0.15 cap for the whole sweep. Live on build d24ed7c475dcd813, lookups for the three real units (the hot tub, the air conditioner and the water heater) verified no successor for any of them, and one candidate for the air conditioner was dropped because its quote did not name the unit's model. The three lookups cost $0.0044 and 8 credits; the four units with invented makers were refused before any call.

  • GetsA cited path to a replacement when parts run out, and a due date from the maker's own interval.
  • WhyA guessed successor sends someone shopping for the wrong unit.
Feature 7

A brief a technician opens with no app

For: the technician

The brief is a single self contained page. Nothing loads from anywhere else, and all model text is escaped so it cannot run as code.

  • GetsA file to text or email, with no login.
  • WhyThe person who needs the brief is not the person who has the tool.
Feature 8

A product recall check that never says the unit is affected

For: the owner, before anyone opens the equipment

The tool asks the free recall service of the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) for every product recall listed under the maker's name, and code keeps only one that prints the unit's exact model. A match shows the product recall's number, title, hazard, remedy and link, and tells the owner to check whether their serial number is in the affected range; it never says the unit itself is affected. With no match it says no product recall names this exact model, with the date checked, never "no recalls." Invented makers are never queried. Run once on the evening of 13 October 2026, on build ad11e6e15a55d30a, 9 free requests returned 12 distinct product recalls for Trane, 6 for Rheem and 1 for Sundance Spas, and none named any of the three models exactly.

  • GetsA safety notice that names this model, with no alarm raised by a similar one.
  • WhyA false match on a safety notice either frightens an owner for nothing or teaches them to ignore the next one.

Part 9

Design decisions, and the safety check

Four design choices shape how memory and replay behave. Each is there for a reason an owner or a technician would recognize. How they came about is recorded in the project's decision log (DECISION-LOG.md in the source).

Decision 6

Store documented causes as well as codes

For: owners of equipment that shows no codes

An air conditioner rarely displays an error code; what its documentation lists is causes, such as a blocked outdoor coil or low refrigerant. So memory keeps a documented cause, with its documented action, under the same word for word evidence rule as a code, and a cause label must share at least 2 content words with its quote. On Haiku 4.5 (build da738a1559ced359), the air conditioner's first lookup stored 6 documented causes and dropped 3 that failed these checks. On build d24ed7c475dcd813 that lookup ended with no reliable answer, so no cause was stored: after the batch, memory holds one fact, the FLO code's meaning on the hot tub, taken from a dealer page.

Decision 7

Never answer a question with no confirmed code from stored codes alone

For: the technician, who needs the full list when there is no code

"Not heating" can have several documented causes. Answering it from the one code memory happens to hold would narrow the list to a single part the symptom never pointed to. Answering from memory alone needs at least 3 distinct verified causes and a match from the symptom classifier; anything less gets a top up of 2 searches. Live on build d24ed7c475dcd813, the vague hot tub question found the FLO code but no stored causes, so the symptom classifier was not called and a short top up ran: 2 searches and 1 fetch. Its limit blocked a second fetch, an 880 Series owner's manual, and the model then declined, naming that manual's troubleshooting section as what was missing: no reliable answer, for $0.0040 (demo case 3). v1 listed six documented possibilities for the same symptom.

Decision 8

Whole word excerpts

For: anyone reading a quote in a brief

Excerpts of a page start and end on word boundaries. A quote cut in the middle of a word can change its meaning, as "$12" cut from "$129" would, and cannot be checked word for word.

Decision 9

Live recordings are replayable offline

For: checking the results

Replay reads the real page text saved from a live run, and marks grounding "unverifiable" when that text was not saved. On the build machine, every live run can be rerun offline for free, and the recorded demo replays the live runs verbatim. The live recordings stay on that machine, out of the public repository, so a reader can check each run ID and build fingerprint in the demo's data file but cannot yet rerun them.

The safety check, by a person's reading

Jev still catches the most safety steps by a person's reading, but with 26 safety steps its lead over the word lists, and over Claude asked the same question, sits inside overlapping 95% intervals, and about half of its flags are steps that person would not call safety steps. The person is me: on 15 October 2026 I labeled the 175 held out steps blind, and the comparison with Claude was registered before it ran. Every other safety result on this page, the live check included, is scored against the labels of three model readers, and by their labels Jev's lead over the published word list is clear. Both sets of results follow: the check and its test against the readers first, then my labels and the Claude comparison.

Why safety steps needed a check in code

The instructions ask the writing model to flag each safety step, and code sorts flagged steps to the top. Code can reorder flagged steps but cannot judge which steps need a flag: deciding what counts as a safety step means either trusting a judgment or writing a rule that judges the step's wording. Nor can code add a step, because every step must cite a source. So a step the model leaves unflagged stays unflagged unless something checks it, and testing such a check was the first item in Part 10.

A safety check in code, tested before it shipped

On 29 and 30 September I tested three ways to catch a safety step the writing model leaves unflagged, each able to add a safety flag but never remove one or add a step: a word rule that flags any step naming certain words (the published list was breaker, power and heat; a tuned list, chosen on the tune half described below, drops the bare word heat but keeps heater and heating, and adds wire, panel, door, disconnect, electrical, jumper and box); Jev, a judgment model from TypeSafe that reads each step and answers how likely it is to be a safety step, as a probability from 0 to 1; and Jev together with the tuned word rule. The test set was 345 steps, taken from maker and dealer documentation and from the tool's own earlier briefs, and labeled before anything was scored by three model readers working blind, who agreed on 338 of them. The set was split into a tune half, where each check's settings were chosen, and a held out half of 175 steps, 31 of them safety steps, which no setting ever saw. The settings were locked in a file before the held out half was scored, once, and the rule for choosing what ships was written before any result: of the tuned word rule, Jev, and Jev together with the tuned word rule, drop any whose precision (the share of its flags that were real safety steps) is below 0.60, then take the highest recall (the share of real safety steps it flagged), then the higher precision. Separately, on 29 September, before the live check, the instruction to the writing model was revised to spell out which steps count as safety steps: power or gas switched off, on or reset; working near anything that can be live, hot or carrying gas; and running or testing a heater.

The second comparison asks whether a general model does Jev's job as well. Claude ran at the cheap classifier setting, effort low, so a higher effort or a larger Claude model might score differently; neither was tried. Rules that stand

  • Speed is reported, not scored.
  • A budget stop never counts as a refusal.
  • The retry is one smaller second pass, taken only if affordable.
  • Misses are published as misses, and each live result is reported only from the build it ran on.

v1's spec named these as out of scope before its build started, which is the only time a cut list is honest. Two came back in v2, for the reason given in Part 4.

  • Accounts, and anything resembling a funnel. Still cut. This is a validation instrument, and email capture would say nothing about the question.
  • Several properties.Back in v2 Memory and service history only pay off per property.
  • Warranty tracking and prevention scheduling.Back in v2 Coverage status is now computed in code from each component's term, and maintenance due from the maker's intervals.
  • Integration with existing operations tools. Still cut. That is the business, not the test.
  • The cleaner capture workflow. Still cut. The right long term entry point, and a second unproven assumption stacked on the first.
  • A name and any branding. Still cut, so nothing rides on presentation.

Out of v2's scope from the start:

  • Paid databases, a hosted graph database, vector stores and hosted servers. None buys anything at this size (Part 5).
  • Repair versus replace dollar amounts. The tool never estimates a price a source does not give.
  • A live demo. The recorded v2 demo replays live runs of 14 October 2026 verbatim and makes no API call, so it is free to visit and free to host.
  • A mobile capture flow, and full mode live.
  • ENERGY STAR efficiency ratings (the U.S. government's efficiency label) and links to technician feedback. Deferred by the second set of requirements.

Appendix A

Method made visible

v1: reviewing my own build adversarially

Before shipping v1 I reviewed the build against its own spec across three lenses: does it match the specification, does it use the platform correctly, and what breaks. A separate pass tried to refute every finding. Thirty five claims, thirty one confirmed. The ones that mattered were an intermittent failure on exactly the search heavy cases the product exists for, output budgets that would have cut a brief off mid sentence, a bug that corrupted citations when reassembling the response, and a script injection hole in the shared brief, the file handed to a third party.

v2: gates, replay first, and a typed "proceed"

  • The build ran in phases. Replay phases came first, at no cost; live phases ran only after a typed "proceed", with the planned calls and spend printed first.
  • The ledger reserves each call before it is made, against a $5 cap for the whole build.
  • Every library name was checked against the current release before use.
  • Planted bugs test the tests: a full run on 15 October 2026, on the code of build 179b4295c98a3143, caught all 1,585.
  • Every run record carries a fingerprint of the code that produced it, so each live v2 result on this page traces to its build: the live evaluation and the demo to build d24ed7c475dcd813, the product recall check to build ad11e6e15a55d30a, Haiku 4.5's comparison figures to builds da738a1559ced359 and 9a64835e86a5a6c8, and the labeled set scores to Jev calls on 30 September under the locked settings and model jev-1.13.0, and the Claude comparison to build 179b4295c98a3143. The whole build spend is the one total that also counts runs on earlier builds.
  • The safety check was scored on a labeled set built before any scoring, with its settings locked and its choice rule written before the held out half was scored once.
  • The check of those labels against a person's ran blind on 15 October 2026. A local labeling page showed me each held out step with the instructions, worked examples and hazard choices the model readers had, and no reader's vote or check's flag; an answer could be changed until sealing, and the latest one stands. The answers were then sealed by a hash (a fingerprint of a file's contents that changes if one byte does) before any score existed. Once they were sealed, the labeling page would not start again, and the scorers check that the labels still match the seal. The 3 answers I changed after seeing the disagreements are kept in a separate file and scored nowhere. The locked settings file and Jev's settings were not touched, and nothing was retuned. Eleven of my yes answers named only "other" as the hazard, which the protocol counts as no, exactly as it counts the readers' votes.
  • The Claude comparison was registered before it ran: its settings were written down and fixed in code (settings fingerprint 658f072743accb13), with Claude Haiku 5.5 asked Jev's exact question and criteria, one call per step, effort low, a single true or false answer, and no thinking, sampling or fallback setting; a true answer is a flag, with no threshold and no tuning; a decline would count as not flagged and be reported apart; one run only, retried only on a lost connection; scored against my sealed labels and the readers' majority, beside Jev and both word lists. It was planned at about $0.02 and capped at $0.15, and cost $0.0182. Its run record names build 179b4295c98a3143, and its spend is ledger run claude-judge-152437a0a5ed, both of 15 October 2026.

What I got wrong

  • v1: I pinned a dependency version from memory instead of checking, and the build failed on the first compile.
  • v1: one of my own tests was wrong. I checked the actual behavior in a browser before changing the assertion.
  • v2: the price check once accepted "$12" from a page that said "$129", caught before any live run.

Honest limits of this write up

  • No technician has used either version.
  • The recorded v2 demo replays live runs of 14 October 2026 with no API call; four of its eight cases end with no reliable answer, as recorded. The four v1 briefs linked in Part 3 remain the record of v1.
  • The v2 property records and warranty rows are synthetic. The property, the owner and the commercial detail are genericized; the equipment and the retrieved documentation are real.
  • The product recall check matches model names, not serial ranges, and has run live once.
  • Cost comparisons with v1 are estimates computed from v1's logged usage, not measurements of v1's bill.
  • The safety step labels came from three model readers and one person, me; no technician has labeled them, and the readers were Claude models, which may favor Claude in the comparison.

Appendix B

Terms used on this page

  • API: a service the code calls over the internet, paid for by use.
  • LangGraph: a library that runs an AI workflow as a graph of steps; each step reads and writes a shared record of the run, and edges decide what runs next.
  • LangChain: a library of building blocks for AI apps: model connectors, tools, and a ready made agent loop.
  • Checkpoint: a saved snapshot of the run, so a run can stop and continue later.
  • Interrupt: a deliberate pause that waits for a person's answer.
  • Middleware: code that wraps every model call or tool call and can check or stop it before it happens.
  • Schema: the exact shape an answer must have: which fields, of which types.
  • Validator: the code that checks each brief against the rules and clears or rejects what fails.
  • Tool calling: the model asking the code to run a tool, here a search or a page fetch, and reading the result.
  • Structured output: the model answers in a fixed, typed form instead of free text.
  • Knowledge graph: facts stored as things and the links between them, each here carrying the exact sentence that supports it.
  • Replay: running the whole pipeline on recorded model replies and search results, at no cost.
  • Ledger: the running record of spend, checked before every paid call.
  • Planted bug: a deliberate small break in the code, used to check that the tests notice it.
  • Token: a word fragment; models are billed per million tokens.
  • Build: a single frozen version of the code, identified by a fingerprint stored in every run record. The live evaluation and the demo ran on build d24ed7c475dcd813, the product recall check on build ad11e6e15a55d30a and the Claude comparison on build 179b4295c98a3143; Haiku 4.5's comparison figures come from builds da738a1559ced359 and 9a64835e86a5a6c8; "the whole build" spend is the project's total.
  • Research effort: a setting for how much the model thinks and how long it keeps researching; high for the research step here.
  • Warranty status: whether each warranty component is active on the run's date, worked out by code from its term and the install or purchase date.
  • Successor model: the model a maker names as replacing a discontinued one.
  • CPSC: the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, which runs a free public service listing product recalls.
  • Product recall match: a product recall that prints the unit's exact model number; a similar model never counts.
  • HVAC: heating, ventilation and air conditioning equipment.
  • Jev: a judgment model from TypeSafe that answers a yes or no question as a probability from 0 to 1; here, whether a step is a safety step.
  • Recall: the share of real safety steps a check flagged.
  • Precision: the share of a check's flags that were real safety steps.
  • Held out half: the half of a labeled set kept aside from every setting choice, so its score is a fair test.
  • Blind labels: labels given without seeing anyone else's labels or any check's flags.
  • Cohen's kappa: agreement between two labelers corrected for the agreement chance alone would give; 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is no better than chance.
  • 95% interval: the range a true rate probably falls in, given how few cases were tested; when two checks' intervals overlap, the test has not shown which is better.
  • Hash: a short fingerprint of a file's contents that changes if one byte changes; sealing the labels by hash shows they were not edited after scoring began.
